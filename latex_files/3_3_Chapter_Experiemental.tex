\chapter{Experimental Setup}

This chapter describes the experimental setup used to collect the data for the long-lived particle searches presented in Sections~\ref{ch:llp_search_dt} and~\ref{ch:llp_search_dedx_dt}. 
All results presented in this thesis are based on data collected by the ATLAS detector at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN.

\section{The Large Hadron Collider}
The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) is currently the world’s largest and highest-energy particle accelerator, with a total circumference of approximately 27~km. 
It is located about 100~m underground, straddling the border between Switzerland and France near Geneva. 
The LHC accelerates protons to nearly the speed of light using radio-frequency (RF) cavities and superconducting magnets, and brings counter-rotating proton beams into collision at four interaction points evenly spaced around the accelerator ring. 
At each interaction point resides one of the four main experiments: ATLAS, CMS, LHCb, and ALICE. 
The center-of-mass energy of proton--proton collisions is 13~TeV during Run~2 and 13.6~TeV during Run~3.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../Pictures/LHC_complex.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of the LHC accelerator complex\cite{Cern_accelerator_complex_2019}, showing the sequence of accelerators used to prepare and inject proton beams into the LHC ring. 
    Image credit: CERN .}
    \label{fig:lhc_complex}
\end{figure}

Protons are produced from hydrogen gas and accelerated through a sequence of injector machines prior to entering the LHC. 
Since hydrogen is initially in molecular form, electrons are first added to form $H^-$ ions. 
These ions are then passed through a stripping foil, removing the electrons and producing protons ($H^+$). 
The protons are initially accelerated by RF cavities and focused using quadrupole magnets in the Linear Accelerator 4 (LINAC4), reaching an energy of 160~MeV.

Subsequently, the proton beam is injected into the Proton Synchrotron Booster (PSB), where it is further accelerated to 2~GeV. 
From there, the beam is transferred through the Proton Synchrotron (PS) and the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS), where it undergoes additional acceleration and bunch shaping before final injection into the LHC. 
A detailed description of the injector chain performance can be found in Ref.~\cite{bartosik2022performancelhcinjectorchain}.

Finally, the prepared proton bunches are injected into the LHC, where they are accelerated to their final energies and brought into collision at the four interaction points.

\section{The ATLAS Detector}

The ATLAS detector is a general purpose particle detector located at one of the four interaction points of the Large Hadron Collider (LHC). 
It is approximately 46~m long, 25~m in diameter, weighs about 7000~tons, and consists of multiple subsystems arranged concentrically around the interaction point.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../Pictures/ATLAS_CrossSection.jpg}
    \caption{Schematic view of the ATLAS detector\cite{ATLAS_Picture}, showing the main subsystems arranged around the interaction point. 
    Image credit: CERN}
    \label{fig:ATLAS_figure}
\end{figure}



Starting from the interaction point and moving radially outward, the main subsystems are the Inner Detector (ID), the central solenoid magnet, the electromagnetic and hadronic calorimeters, the toroidal magnet system, and the Muon Spectrometer (MS). 
Each subsystem is designed to measure specific properties of particles produced in proton--proton collisions.

The Inner Detector is the closest subsystem to the interaction point and is responsible for reconstructing the trajectories of charged particles. 
It provides precise measurements of track positions and momenta within a 2~T magnetic field generated by the central solenoid, which surrounds the ID. 
Outside the solenoid lies the electromagnetic calorimeter, which measures the energies of electrons and photons through the development of electromagnetic showers. 
Surrounding this is the hadronic calorimeter, designed to measure the energies of hadrons via hadronic showering, working in conjunction with the electromagnetic calorimeter to provide a complete measurement of the event energy flow.

Enclosing the calorimeters is the toroidal magnet system, which supplies the magnetic field required for the Muon Spectrometer. 
The MS forms the outermost layer of ATLAS and is optimized for the detection and momentum measurement of muons. 
Due to their relatively large mass, long lifetime, and weak interactions with matter, muons typically traverse the inner detector and calorimeters with minimal energy loss, making the MS essential for their identification. 
The MS employs a combination of tracking chambers and magnetic fields to measure the curvature of muon trajectories, enabling precise momentum determination.




The ATLAS detector uses a right handed coordinate system. 
The origin is at the nominal interaction point with the Z axis along the beam pipe. 
The Y axis points upwards and the X axis points to the center of the LHC ring.
In ATLAS the normal angles $\theta$ and $\phi$ are used where $\theta$ is the polar angle measured from the positive z axis and $\phi$ is the azimuthal angle measured in the x-y plane from the x axis.
One important variable used in ATLAS is pseudorapidity, $\eta$, which is defined as $\eta = -ln(tan(\theta/2))$ where $\theta$ is the polar angle measured from the positive z axis.
Pseudorapidity is useful for ATLAS as it is invariant under Lorentz boosts along the beam axis with the assumption that the particles momentum is much larger then mass, $pc\gg mc^2$.

\subsection{Inner Detector}

The inner detector (ID) is a marvel of technology and innovation that was used in collaboration
with other detector systems on ATLAS to not only discover a new particle but to confirm that it is
a scalar Higgs Boson. The ID is at the center of the ATLAS detector and measures momentum,
position, impact parameter, charge, and some particle identification including via ionization loss,
of charged particles that pass though it.
The ID consists of three main components: the Pixel Detector, the Semiconductor Tracker (SCT), and the Transition Radiation Tracker (TRT).

Particles that transverse the Pixel Detector, the SCT, and the TRT have momentum resolution which can be parameterized as


\begin{equation}
\frac{\sigma_{p_\mathrm{T}}}{p_\mathrm{T}} = 0.05\% \times p_\mathrm{T}~[\mathrm{GeV}] \oplus 1\%.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Pixel Detector}

The Pixel Detector is located closest to the interaction point and is composed of multiple layers of silicon pixel sensors, providing charged-particle tracking over radial distances from 33.5~mm to 122.5~mm. 
It consists of the Insertable B-Layer (IBL), three barrel layers, and three endcap disks on each side of the detector. 
This configuration provides up to four space-point measurements for charged particles within a pseudorapidity coverage of $|\eta| < 2.5$.

The innermost pixel layer, known as the Insertable B-Layer (IBL), was installed during the first long shutdown of the LHC to enhance tracking and vertexing performance. 
The IBL is positioned at a radius of 33.5~mm from the beamline, enabling high-precision measurements close to the interaction point. 
It is constructed from 14 staves, each containing 32 front-end chips mounted on silicon sensors.

Two sensor technologies are employed in the IBL: planar sensors and 3D sensors. 
Planar sensors are used in the central region of each stave, while 3D sensors are utilized in the forward regions due to their superior radiation tolerance.
The superior radiation tolerance of 3D sensors vs planar sensors comes from a reduction in the electrode separation which reduces the probability of charge trapping. 
Each sensor is segmented into pixels with dimensions of $50~\mu\text{m}$ in $\phi$ and $250~\mu\text{m}$ in $z$. 
Each stave contains 80 columns and 336 rows of pixels, resulting in approximately 12 million pixels in the full IBL.

The remaining three barrel layers of the Pixel Detector, referred to as the B-layer, Layer~1, and Layer~2, are located at radii of 50.5~mm, 88.5~mm, and 122.5~mm, respectively. 
In these layers, planar silicon sensors with a thickness of $250~\mu\text{m}$ are used, with pixel dimensions of $50~\mu\text{m}$ in $\phi$ and $3  00~\mu\text{m}$ in $z$.

The pixel detector is capped by 3 endcaps on either side of the collision point at z distances of 495, 580, 650


A pixel module consists of a silicon sensor bump-bonded to dedicated readout chips. 
The sensors are based on reverse-biased silicon diodes. 
Applying a reverse bias voltage creates a depleted region within the sensor volume. 
Charged particles traversing this depletion region generate approximately 90 electron-hole pairs per micrometer, with each pair requiring about 3.6~eV to be produced\cite{Particle_Detectors}. 
The resulting charge carriers drift under the applied electric field, inducing a measurable signal on the electrodes.

Direct measurement of the collected charge would require power-intensive analog-to-digital conversion. 
Instead, the pixel readout employs a digital scheme in which the induced signal is first amplified and shaped, and then compared to a predefined threshold. 
The duration for which the signal remains above this threshold, referred to as the Time-over-Threshold (ToT), is proportional to the deposited charge. 
Due to the non-linear response of the electronics, calibration is performed by injecting known charges and measuring the corresponding ToT response.
 

\subsubsection{Semiconductor Tracker}

The Semiconductor Tracker (SCT) is located outside the Pixel Detector and provides precision tracking at radial distances between approximately 299~mm and 560~mm from the beamline, covering a pseudorapidity range of $|\eta| < 2.5$. 
The SCT is a silicon microstrip detector designed to complement the Pixel Detector by extending the tracking lever arm and improving momentum resolution.

The SCT consists of 4088 silicon strip modules arranged in four barrel layers and two endcaps, each comprising nine disks. 
Each module contains two back-to-back silicon strip sensors mounted with a small stereo angle, enabling three-dimensional space-point reconstruction.

\subsubsection{Transition Radiation Tracker}

The Transition Radiation Tracker (TRT) forms the outermost component of the Inner Detector. 
It is composed of straw tubes filled with a xenon-based gas mixture, which serve both as tracking detectors and as transition radiation detectors for electron identification.
 

As charged particles traverse the straw tubes, they ionize the gas, and the resulting electrons drift toward the central anode wires where the signals are read out. 
Each straw has a diameter of 4~mm and can provide up to approximately 36 measurements per track, significantly increasing the number of tracking points in the outer region of the Inner Detector.

A limitation of the TRT is that it provides position measurements only in the $R$--$\phi$ plane. 
Nevertheless, when combined with the Pixel Detector and SCT, the TRT enhances pattern recognition and improves track reconstruction, particularly in dense environments.




\subsection{Calorimeters}

ATLAS employs two main calorimeter systems: the electromagnetic calorimeter and the hadronic calorimeter. 
Together, these systems are designed to absorb  and measure the energies of particles emerging from the interaction point after they traverse the Inner Detector.

\subsubsection{Electromagnetic Calorimeter}

The electromagnetic calorimeter is a sampling calorimeter that uses lead as the absorber material and liquid argon as the active medium. 
Incident electrons and photons initiate electromagnetic showers in the lead absorbers, with the resulting ionization signals collected in the liquid argon layers to provide precise energy measurements.

The electromagnetic calorimeter is divided into a barrel section and two endcap sections. 
The barrel region covers pseudorapidities of $|\eta| < 1.475$, while the endcap regions extend the coverage to $1.375 < |\eta| < 3.2$.

\subsubsection{Hadronic Calorimeter}

The hadronic calorimeter is also a sampling calorimeter, employing steel and copper as absorber materials and scintillating tiles and liquid argon as active media. 
It is designed to measure the energies of hadrons through the development of hadronic showers.

Similar to the electromagnetic calorimeter, the hadronic calorimeter is divided into a barrel section and two endcap sections. 
The barrel region covers $|\eta| < 1.4$, while the endcap regions extend the coverage to $1.4 < |\eta| < 4.8$. 
In the forward and endcap regions, liquid argon technology is used to accommodate the higher radiation levels. 

\subsection{Muon Spectrometer}

Due to their relatively large mass and weak interactions with matter, muons typically traverse the Inner Detector and calorimeters while depositing only small amounts of ionization energy. 
As a result, the outermost subsystem of ATLAS is dedicated to reconstructing the momentum and trajectory of these weakly interacting charged particles.

The Muon Spectrometer is constructed using five detector technologies: Thin Gap Chambers (TGCs), Resistive Plate Chambers (RPCs), Monitored Drift Tubes (MDTs), Small-Strip Thin Gap Chambers (sTGCs), and Micromegas. 
The majority of the precision tracking measurements are provided by the Monitored Drift Tubes.

The Muon Spectrometer consists of a barrel region and two endcap regions. 
The barrel region covers the pseudorapidity range $|\eta| < 1.2$, while the endcap regions extend the coverage to $1.0 < |\eta| < 2.7$.
