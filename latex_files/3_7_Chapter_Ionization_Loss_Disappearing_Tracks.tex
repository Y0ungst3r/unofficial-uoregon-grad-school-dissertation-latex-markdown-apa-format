This analysis searches for heavy long-lived charged particles using disappearing track signatures characterized by high ionization energy loss ($dE/dx$). Such signatures are predicted in several BSM theories, including GMSB and AMSB. In these scenarios, long-lived particles arise naturally due to small mass splittings or weak couplings. 
Given the current limits on SUSY~\cite{Atlas_SUSY_Limits}, low mass SUSY particles are largely excluded, motivating the search for heavier particles. 


A disappearing track is a reconstructed pixel track that hits at least the first four pixel layers of the Inner Detector but doesn't hit the required number to be a full track. 
These tracks are associated with three physical phenomena, the decay of the particle, scattering of the particle, or uncorrelated hits that are misreconstructed as a track.
These short tracks, called tracklets, provide a unique experimental signature not common from standard model particles.

There are three sources of background in the disappearing track searches, fakes, hadronic scattering, and leptonic scattering.
These three sources of background nominally have low$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$, however, due to the statistical nature of ionization energy loss can have large$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$. 






\section{Data}
\label{sec:Data_EP}


Full run 2 and partial run 3 data is used in this search. 
The data is from PP collisions at a center of mass energy for run 2 of $S = \sqrt{13 TeV}$ and for run 3 of $S = \sqrt{13.6 TeV}$.
The total integrated lumonisity used for this search is about $300 fb^{-1}$. 

The datasets used were produced using a non-standard reconstruction chain.
This analysis requires information not retained in standard DAODs and is typically only available in the RAW data, such as pixel cluster charge, size and shape.
To retain these non-standard variables in the DAOD, event picking was used to select the data. 
Event picking is a method for selecting specific events to be reconstructed in a non-standard reconstruction chain, allowing great flexibility in choosing what information is saved.

Event picking relies on the EventIndex, A system designed for direct selection and retrieval of individual events. 
By retrieving only the required subset of the full dataset, the number of files that must be staged from tape is significantly reduced, decreasing the total reprocessing time. 
Event picking consists of three main steps.

\textbf{Event selection.} \\
A subset of the total dataset to be reconstructed must be identified.
This is done by analyzing the standard DAOD files produced in the nominal reconstruction chain to determine which events are relevant for the analysis. 
The selected events include those in the signal region, validation regions, control regions, and additional events required for dedicated studies.
Once the events are identified, their corresponding run and event numbers are extracted. 

\textbf{Event retrieval}\\
The selected event and run numbers, together with any needed metadata(E.G. trigger stream information or run years), are provided to the event picking service.
The event picking service queries the EventIndex and retrieves the corresponding RAW events. 
These events are then collected into containers that can be managed via rucio and stored in group storage.  

\textbf{Event reconstruction}\\
The selected RAW events are reconstructed using a dedicated reconstruction designed to preserve information that is normally not saved during the reconstruction stage. 
These DAODs are then compared to the datasets from the nominal reconstruction chain to check for issues in the reconstruction and to ensure that all selected events are present in the DAODs.


The event selections used for event picking are defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:event_picking_selections}.




\section{Simulation}
\label{sec:Simulation}
The Monte Carlo (MC) samples used probe a largely unexplored region of parameter space in searches for physics BSM.
The targeted phase space corresponds to heavy particles with masses of $\mathcal{O}(100$--$1000~\GeV)$ and lifetimes of $\mathcal{O}(0.1$--$10~ns)$.
This search targets the direct detection of long-lived charged particles and is therefore designed to be as model-agnostic as reasonably possible, allowing future reinterpretations of other BSM models more straight forward.
The benchmark models considered are motivated by SUSY, which naturally provides mechanisms for long-lived particles.
In particular, the analysis focuses on GMSB stau scenarios and AMSB wino scenarios.


\subsection{Wino}

For the wino benchmark, the SUSY scenario considered is AMSB.
In this framework, the lightest neutralino is typically the LSP, while the chargino (wino-like) is the NLSP, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:feynman_wino}.
The small mass splitting between the chargino and the neutralino leads to a suppressed phase space for the chargino decay, resulting in a long lifetime.

The wino samples are generated using a simplified AMSB-inspired model in which only the masses and lifetimes of the charginos and neutralinos are varied to determine the kinematics.
This approach assumes that the slepton masses are sufficiently large to be neglected.
The simplified model has been validated against full AMSB simulations and shows good agreement.
Although the branching ratio of the chargino must be set manually in this framework, the simplified setup significantly streamlines the generation of samples.

The wino samples were generated using \textsc{MadGraph5\_aMC@NLO}~v3.5.1 interfaced with \textsc{Pythia}~8.308 and \textsc{EvtGen}~v2.1.1.
The A14 tune with the NNPDF23LO PDF set was used.

\subsection{Stau}

The stau benchmark is based on a GMSB scenario.
In this case, the gravitino is the LSP and is massless.
The stau is the NLSP and decays to a tau lepton and a gravitino, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:feynman_stau}.
Due to the very small coupling to the gravitino, the stau can acquire a long lifetime.

The stau samples were generated using \textsc{MadGraph5\_aMC@NLO}~v3.3.1 interfaced with \textsc{Pythia}~8.308 and \textsc{EvtGen}~v2.1.1.
The A14 tune with the NNPDF23LO PDF set was used.

\section{Signal Region and Selections}


To achieve optimal sensitivity across a range of signal models and mass scales, this analysis defines four signal regions, grouped into two categories: decay agnostic and decay specific.
The decay agnostic regions are designed to remain largely independent of the underlying decay mode, focusing instead on the identification of disappearing tracks with high ionization energy loss. These regions are further subdivided into two categories targeting high mass and low mass particles, respectively.
In contrast, the decay specific regions explicitly target particular decay signatures. One region selects events containing a kinked tracklet, indicative of a decay, while the other applies a veto on such kinks to target models with soft decay products that cannot be reconstructed.
Together, these signal regions provide broad and complementary sensitivity to multiple models while enabling more robust future reinterpretations of the results. A schematic overview of the regions is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sr_diagram}.

\label{sec:event_picking_selections}

As described in Sec.~\ref{sec:Data_EP}, not all events can be reconstructed with the full cluster information. Therefore, an event picking procedure is applied. To efficiently utilize this procedure, a set of preliminary selections is defined with the goal of retaining approximately $10^6$ events. These selected events include signal, validation, and control regions.


The selection strategy proceeds in stages. First, poorly reconstructed tracklets are removed using quality criteria. Subsequently, requirements on MET, $p_T$, and track based isolation are applied to suppress background contributions while retaining signal efficiency. 

A key aspect of this strategy is that certain potentially powerful background rejection selections are intentionally not applied at this stage. Instead, they are reserved to define dedicated validation and control regions, ensuring robust background modeling and cross checks. The full set of selections is summarized in Tab. ~\ref{tab:EP_selections}.

The variables MET, $p_T$, and track based isolation are found to provide the most effective discrimination between signal and background while maintaining high signal acceptance.

To validate these choices, the impact of the MET, $p_T$, and track-based isolation requirements on both signal efficiency and background rejection were evaluated, results can be seen in Fig. \ref{fig:selection_performance}

\textbf{Tracklet Momentum}\\
It is observed that $p_T$ exhibits a rapidly falling distribution for background, whereas the signal exhibits a broad, smeared peak at higher $p_T$, with a strong suppression of events at low $p_T$. Motivating the selections shown in Fig. ~\ref{fig:selection_performance}

\textbf{Event Missing Transverse Energy}\\
MeT is used for removing a significant number of background events. The MET requirement is observed to alter the relative composition of background processes, necessitating a selection to suppress the QCD contributions in the low MeT region. In addition, the chosen MET threshold preserves a low MET validation region for background estimation and is therefore lower than the expected optimal value for background rejection and signal acceptance. The final MeT selection is further optimized within each signal region using cluster level selections. The distributions of MET for signal and background are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:selection_performance}.

\textbf{Track Isolation}\\
The last major selection used to remove background is track level isolation. The track level isolation is found to remove hadronic decays and jets efficiently without removing signal. This can be seen in Fig. ~\ref{fig:selection_performance}.

The full cutflow for event picking can be seen in Fig. ~\ref{fig:Ep_cutflow}

\section{Background Estimation}


For this search there are two primary sources of backgrounds, fakes and scattering background. 

Fakes are mis-combination of multiple real low-momentum particles that are reconstructed as a single high-momentum tracklet. 
These fake tracklets can exhibit high ionization energy loss as the underlying particles interacting with the sensors are low-momentum and therefore can have a large ionization energy loss. 
Fake tracklets are a challenging background as they originate from mis-combinations of particles and are therefore heavily influenced by pileup conditions and detector performance.
As a result they are difficult to reliably model the abundance using Monte Carlo simulation. 

The scattering background originates from real high-energy electrons, muons, or hadrons that undergo material interactions, or more rarely decay, such that they produce a tracklet signature. 
Although these particles are typically minimum ionizing at the relevant energies, the statistical nature of ionization energy loss, as well as occasional overlaps with other particles, can cause them to appear as highly ionizing. 
Consequently, the scattering background is strongly influenced by the detector material and detector conditions, making accurate modeling in Monte Carlo simulation challenging.

For this analysis, two background estimation methods have been developed in parallel.
This approach allows different estimation strategies to be tested and validated against each other.
Due to the challenging conditions that lead to the background sources in this analysis both techniques are fully data driven. 
The first technique developed is a simple cut and count method relying on pt and dE/dx.
The second technique is a two component dE/dx shape fit.
Both methods have been developed together and use the same baseline selections described in section ~\ref{sec:baseline_selections}

\subsection{ABCD Method in dE/dx and pT}
\label{sec:Background_Estimation_ABCD_in_dEdx_and_pT}

For the ABCD background estimates pT and dE/dx are used to define four different regions. 
The signal regions and three control regions are constructed using simple threshold selections in $p_T$ and$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$. 
The expected background yield in the signal region is predicted from the control regions according to Eq. ~\ref{eq:ABCD}.
This estimation assumes that pT and dE/dx are uncorrelated.
\begin{equation}
    N_A = \frac{N_B \cdot N_C}{N_D}
    \label{eq:ABCD}
\end{equation}


To ensure the validity of this background assumption the correlation between $p_T$ and dE/dx is studied.
The correlation in background between $p_T$ an$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ is evaluated in two ways. 
First is by directly comparing the dE/dx shapes for different values of dE/dx. 
The other is by slicing the data in $p_T$ and examining the fraction of events in the$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ tails. 
At higher dE/dx values there is few events so uneven bins were choosen. 
The results can be seen in Fig. \ref{fig:tail_frac} and \ref{fig:dEdx_shapes}.
The tail fractions are roughly flat across pT bins showing us that the landau tail is not changing at higher pT values. 
The$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ distributuion is constant showing us that the$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ distribution is not changing.

The background estimation is validated in several independent regions designed to test closure and correlation with different background compositions:
\begin{itemize}

    \item a minimum-ionizing region
    \item a fake enriched region

\end{itemize}
The results of these regions as well as the signal region can be seen in fig~\ref{tab:Inclusive_VR_ABCD_reformatted}


To further study the validity of the ABCD method and the behaior of the dE/dx tails, a simple toy model of the signal region is constructed. 
The toy model is built using templets extracted from data. a dE/dx templates is obtained from the low momentum, while the pT template is obtained from the low-dEdx region. These templates are then used a PDF for the randomly generate pseudo events to be selected from. 
Using the generated-events, a simple cut and count procedure is applied to estimate the expected yield in the signal region. This toy model provides a consistency check of the ABCD prediction and aloows the impact of the dE/dx tails to be further studied.

\subsection{Two component$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ template}
The two component$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ background estimation is done using the$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ as the final discriminate. This is done by extracting the background$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ templates from specific validation regions and then appyyinh the component fits to extract the background estimation in the signal regions. 
This method relies on the assumptiin that background consists of only two unique shapes, fakes and MIPs. 
The background fit is then simplified to two parameters and the resulting background pdf is given by Eq.~\ref{eq:pdf_background}.

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{B-only}} = N_{\mathrm{CR}} \cdot \left( f_{\mathrm{fake}} \cdot \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{fake}} + (1-f_{\mathrm{fake}}) \cdot \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{MIP}} \right)\, .
    \label{eq:pdf_background}
\end{equation}

Where the indivuidal $\mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{fake}}$ and $\mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{MIP}}$ are extracted from dedicated control regions and the parameters $N_{\mathrm{CR}} $ and $f_{\mathrm{fake}}$ are left as the component fits.

\subsubsection{Template Extraction: MIPs}

The MIP template, $\mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{MIP}}$, is extracted from multiple MIP enhanced regions. There are three sources of MIPs: electrons, muons, and hadrons. The MIP $dE/dx$ shape is extracted individually for each source. The resulting $dE/dx$ shapes are consistent across the different background sources.

Electrons, muons, and hadrons exhibit the same $dE/dx$ shape, indicating that all three background sources are well described by a common MIP $dE/dx$ template. This can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:MIP_Templates}. The final extracted MIP template is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:MIP_template}.

\subsection{Template Extraction: Fakes}

Fakes do not have a similar $dE/dx$ shape to other sources of background due to multiple effects. The reconstructed path length of fake tracklets is miscalculated because the particles producing the hits do not necessary follow the trajectory of the reconstructed tracklet. In addition, fakes can include hits from low-momentum, high-$dE/dx$ particles that are not typically reconstructed.

These effects result in a unique $dE/dx$ shape that must be extracted separately from other background sources. The fake template, $\mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{fake}}$, is extracted from a fake enriched region defined by the selections in Tab.~\ref{tab:fake_enriched_region}. The resulting template is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fake_template}.
