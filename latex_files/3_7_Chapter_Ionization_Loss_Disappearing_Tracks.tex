\chapter{Disappearing Tracks with High Ionization Energy Loss}
\label{ch:llp_search_dedx_dt}
\section{Analysis Introduction}

This analysis searches for heavy long-lived charged particles using disappearing track signatures characterized by high ionization energy loss ($\text{d}E/\text{d}x$). Such signatures are predicted in several BSM theories, including GMSB and AMSB SUSY as discussed in Sec. \ref{sec:CMSSM_stau} and \ref{sec:GMSB}. In these scenarios, long-lived particles arise naturally due to small mass splittings or weak couplings. 
Given the current limits on SUSY~\cite{Atlas_SUSY_Limits}, low mass, 200 GeV and lower, SUSY particles are largely excluded, motivating the search for heavier particles. 


A disappearing track is a reconstructed pixel track that hits at least the first four pixel layers of the Inner Detector but doesn't hit the required number of silicon layers to be reconstructed as a track. 
These tracks are associated with three physical phenomena: the decay of a particle, scattering of a particle, or uncorrelated hits that are reconstructed as a track.
These short tracks, called tracklets, provide a unique experimental signature not common from standard model particles.

There are three sources of background in the disappearing track searches, fakes, hadronic scattering, and leptonic scattering.
These three sources of background nominally have low $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$; however, due to the statistical nature of ionization energy loss can have large $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$. 






\section{Data}
\label{sec:Data_EP}


Full run 2 and partial run 3 data is used in this search. 
The data is from \pp collisions at a center of mass energy for run 2 of $\sqrt{s} = 13 \TeV$ and for run 3 of $\sqrt{s} = 13.6 \TeV$.
The total integrated luminosity used for this search is about $300\, \text{fb}^{-1}$. 

The datasets used were produced using a non-standard reconstruction chain.
This analysis requires information not retained in standard DAODs and is typically only available in the RAW data, such as pixel cluster charge, size and shape; 
normally, these values are dropped to reduce the size of the AOD files and reduce computation time. 
Dropping cluster information is a reasonable choice for ATLAS as a whole as cluster level information is not needed in the vast majority of physics analyses, not even among the long-lived particle searches. 
To retain these non-standard variables in the DAOD, event picking was used to select the data. 
Event picking is a method for selecting specific events to be reconstructed in a non-standard reconstruction chain, allowing great flexibility in choosing what information is saved.

Event picking relies on the EventIndex system designed for direct selection and retrieval of individual events. 
By retrieving only the required subset of the full dataset, the number of files that must be staged from tape is significantly reduced, decreasing the total reprocessing time. 
Event picking consists of three main steps.

\subsubsection{Event selection}
A subset of the total dataset to be reconstructed must be identified.
This is done by analyzing the standard DAOD files produced in the nominal reconstruction chain to determine which events are relevant for the analysis. 
The selected events include those in the signal region, validation regions, control regions, and additional events required for dedicated studies.
Once the events are identified, their corresponding run and event numbers are extracted. 

The event selections used for event picking are defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:event_picking_selections}.

\subsubsection{Event retrieval}
The selected event and run numbers, together with any needed metadata (E.G. trigger stream information or run years), are provided to the event picking service.
The event picking service queries the EventIndex and retrieves the corresponding RAW events. 
These events are then collected into containers that can be managed via rucio and stored in group storage.  

\subsubsection{Event reconstruction}
The selected RAW events are reconstructed using a dedicated reconstruction designed to preserve information that is normally not saved during the reconstruction stage. 
These DAODs are then compared to the datasets from the nominal reconstruction chain to check for issues in the reconstruction and to ensure that all selected events are present in the DAODs.






\section{Simulation}
\label{sec:Simulation}
The Monte Carlo (MC) samples used probe a largely unexplored region of parameter space in searches for physics BSM.
The targeted phase space corresponds to heavy particles with masses of $\mathcal{O}(100$--$1000~\GeV)$ and lifetimes of $\mathcal{O}(0.1$--$10~\text{ns})$.
This search targets the direct detection of long-lived charged particles and is therefore designed to be as model-agnostic as reasonably possible, allowing future reinterpretations of other BSM models more straight forward.
The benchmark models considered are motivated by SUSY, which naturally provides mechanisms for long-lived particles.
In particular, the analysis focuses on GMSB stau scenarios and AMSB wino scenarios.


\subsection{Wino}

For the wino benchmark, the SUSY scenario considered is AMSB.
In this framework, the lightest neutralino is typically the LSP, while the chargino (wino-like) is the NLSP, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:feynman_wino}.
The small mass splitting between the chargino and the neutralino leads to a suppressed phase space for the chargino decay, resulting in a long lifetime.

The wino samples are generated using a simplified AMSB inspired model in which only the masses and lifetimes of the charginos and neutralinos are varied to determine the kinematics.
This approach assumes that the slepton masses are sufficiently large to be neglected.
The simplified model has been validated against full AMSB simulations and shows good agreement.
Although the branching ratio of the chargino must be set manually in this framework, the simplified setup significantly streamlines the generation of samples.

The wino samples were generated using \textsc{MadGraph5\_aMC@NLO}~v3.5.1 interfaced with \textsc{Pythia}~8.308 and \textsc{EvtGen}~v2.1.1.
The A14 tune with the NNPDF23LO PDF set was used.

\subsection{Stau}

The stau benchmark is based on a GMSB scenario.
In this case, the gravitino is the LSP and nearly massless.
The stau is the NLSP and decays to a tau lepton and a gravitino, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:feynman_stau}.
Due to the very small coupling to the gravitino, the stau can acquire a long lifetime.

The stau samples were generated using \textsc{MadGraph5\_aMC@NLO}~v3.3.1 interfaced with \textsc{Pythia}~8.308 and \textsc{EvtGen}~v2.1.1.
The A14 tune with the NNPDF23LO PDF set was used.

\section{Signal Region and Selections}


To achieve optimal sensitivity across a range of signal models and mass scales, this analysis defines four signal regions, grouped into two categories: decay-agnostic and decay-specific.
The decay-agnostic regions are designed to remain largely independent of the underlying decay mode, focusing instead on the identification of disappearing tracks with high ionization energy loss. These regions are further subdivided into two categories targeting high mass and low mass particles, respectively.
In contrast, the decay-specific regions explicitly target particular decay signatures. One region selects events containing a kinked tracklet, indicative of a decay, while the other applies a veto on such kinks to target models with soft decay products that cannot be reconstructed.
Together, these signal regions provide broad and complementary sensitivity to multiple models while enabling more robust future reinterpretations of the results. A schematic overview of the regions is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sr_diagram}.


\subsection{Event Picking}
\label{sec:event_picking_selections}
To further exploit the discriminating power of ionization energy loss, it is necessary to incorporate cluster level information in the event reconstruction. This additional granularity improves the performance of $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$-based selections, motivating the need for event picking strategy.

As described in Sec.~\ref{sec:Data_EP}, not all events can be reconstructed with the full cluster information. Therefore, an event picking procedure is applied. To efficiently utilize this procedure, a set of preliminary selections is defined with the goal of retaining approximately $10^6$ events. These selected events include signal, validation, and control regions.


The selection strategy proceeds in stages. First, poorly reconstructed tracklets are removed using quality criteria. Subsequently, requirements on MET, $p_T$, and track based isolation are applied to suppress background contributions while retaining signal efficiency. 

A key aspect of this strategy is that certain potentially powerful background rejection selections are intentionally not applied at this stage. Instead, they are reserved to define dedicated validation and control regions, ensuring robust background modeling and cross-checks. The full set of selections is summarized in Tab. ~\ref{tab:EP_selections}.

The variables MET, $p_T$, and track based isolation are found to provide the most effective discrimination between signal and background while maintaining high signal acceptance.
The impact of the MET, $p_T$, and track-based isolation requirements on both signal efficiency and background rejection were evaluated, results can be seen in Fig. \ref{fig:selection_performance}.

\subsubsection{Tracklet Momentum}
It is observed that $p_T$ exhibits a rapidly falling distribution for background, whereas the signal exhibits a broad, smeared peak at higher $p_T$, with a strong suppression of events at low $p_T$. Motivating the selections shown in Fig. ~\ref{fig:selection_performance}

\subsubsection{Event Missing Transverse Energy}
MET is used for removing a significant number of background events. The MET requirement is observed to alter the relative composition of background processes. At MET $< 100\,\GeV$ QCD contributions begin to dominate the events selected, thus necessitating a MET $> 100\,\GeV$ for all studies. In addition, the chosen MET threshold preserves a low MET validation region for background estimation and is therefore lower than the expected optimal value for background rejection and signal acceptance. The final MET selection is further optimized within each signal region using cluster level selections. The distributions of MET for signal and background are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:selection_performance}.

\subsubsection{Track Isolation}
The last major selection used to remove background is track level isolation. The track level isolation is found to remove hadronic decays and jets efficiently without removing signal. This can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:selection_performance}.

The full cutflow for event picking can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:Ep_cutflow}

\section{Background Estimation}


For this search there are two primary sources of backgrounds,  fakes and scattering backgrounds. 

Fakes are mis-combinations of hits from multiple real low-momentum particles that are reconstructed as a single high-momentum tracklet. 
These fake tracklets can exhibit high ionization energy loss as the underlying particles interacting with the sensors can be low-momentum and therefore can have a large ionization energy loss. 
Fake tracklets are a challenging background as they originate from mis-combinations of particles and are therefore heavily influenced by pileup conditions and detector performance.
As a result it is difficult to reliably model the abundance using Monte Carlo simulation. 

The scattering background originates from real high-energy electrons, muons, or hadrons that undergo material interactions, or more rarely decay, such that they produce a tracklet signature. 
Although these particles are typically minimum ionizing at the relevant energies, the statistical nature of ionization energy loss, as well as occasional overlaps with other particles, can cause them to appear as highly ionizing. 
Consequently, the scattering background is strongly influenced by the detector material and detector conditions, making accurate modeling in Monte Carlo simulation challenging.

For this analysis, two background estimation methods have been developed in parallel.
Due to the challenging conditions that lead to the background sources in this analysis both techniques are fully data driven. 
The first technique developed is a ABCD method relying on $p_T$ and $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$.
The second technique is a two component $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ shape fit.
Both methods have been developed together and use the same baseline selections described in Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline_selections}
This approach allows different estimation strategies to be tested and validated against each other.
Additionally, the two component $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ shape fit requires large number of statistics in the SR while the ABCD method retains sensitivity with much lower number of statistics regions.
\subsection{ABCD Method in $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ and $p_T$}
\label{sec:Background_Estimation_ABCD_in_dEdx_and_pT}

For the ABCD background estimates $p_T$ and $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ are used to define four different regions. 
The signal regions and three control regions are constructed using simple threshold selections in $p_T$ and $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$. 
The expected background yield in the signal region is predicted from the control regions according to Eq. ~\ref{eq:ABCD}.
This estimation assumes that $p_T$ and $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ are uncorrelated.
\begin{equation}
    N_A = \frac{N_B \cdot N_C}{N_D}
    \label{eq:ABCD}
\end{equation}


To validate the assumption that the background $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ distribution is independent of $p_T$, the correlation between these two variables is studied. This correlation is evaluated in a low-MET control region; the low-MET control region has the same selections as baseline except, $80\,\GeV < \text{MET} < 100\,\GeV$. The low-MET control region is expected to be dominated by background processes.

Two complementary approaches are used to test this assumption. First, the $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ distributions are directly compared across different $p_T$ bins to examine whether the overall shape varies with transverse momentum. Second, the data are divided into $p_T$ slices and the fraction of events in the high-$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ tail is evaluated in each bin. Since relatively few events populate the high-$\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ region, non-uniform binning is used to ensure sufficient statistical precision across the full range.

The results of these studies are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:tail_frac} and Fig. \ref{fig:dEdx_shapes}. The tail fractions are observed to be consistent with a flat distribution across $p_T$ bins within statistical uncertainties, indicating that the Landau tail does not exhibit significant momentum dependence. Similarly, the $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ distributions remain stable across the full $p_T$ range. These results support the assumption that the background $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ distribution is effectively independent of $p_T$, validating its use in the background estimation procedure.

The background estimation is validated in several independent regions designed to test closure and correlation with different background compositions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A low-$p_T$ region between 20 and 60 GeV
    \item a minimum-ionizing region
    \item a fake enriched region
    \item a low-$E^{miss}_T$ region between 100 and 120 GeV
\end{itemize}
The results of these regions as well as the signal region can be seen in fig~\ref{tab:Inclusive_VR_ABCD_reformatted}


To further study the behavior of the $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ tails and optimize the signal region selection used in the ABCD method, a template-based toy model of the expected background in the signal region is constructed. 
The toy model is built using templates extracted from data after the baseline event selections have been applied. A $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ template is obtained from the low-momentum control region, defined as $p_T < 50\,\GeV$, while the $p_T$ template is extracted from the low-$\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ control region, defined as $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x < 1.2\,\MeV\,\mathrm{g}^{-1}\,\mathrm{cm}^{2}$. These templates are normalized to probability distribution functions and are sampled independently to generate pseudo-events, with each event assigned a randomly sampled $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x$ and $p_T$ value.

The generated pseudo-events are then used in a cut-and-count procedure to estimate the expected background yield in the signal region. By scanning over different signal region boundary definitions, the predicted background yields can be compared with simulated signal expectations to optimize sensitivity across a range of signal models. In addition, comparison of the toy model prediction with the nominal ABCD estimate provides a consistency check of the background estimation procedure.

\subsection{Two component $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ template}
The two component $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ background estimation is done using the $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ as the final discriminate. This is done by extracting the background $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ templates from specific validation regions and then applying the component fits to extract the background estimation in the signal regions. 
This method relies on the assumption that background consists of only two unique shapes, fakes and MIPs. 
The background fit is then simplified to two parameters and the resulting background pdf is given by Eq.~\ref{eq:pdf_background}.

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{B-only}} = N_{\mathrm{CR}} \cdot \left( f_{\mathrm{fake}} \cdot \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{fake}} + (1-f_{\mathrm{fake}}) \cdot \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{MIP}} \right)\, .
    \label{eq:pdf_background}
\end{equation}

Where the indivuidal $\mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{fake}}$ and $\mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{MIP}}$ are extracted from dedicated control regions and the parameters $N_{\mathrm{CR}} $ and $f_{\mathrm{fake}}$ are left as the component fits.

\subsubsection{Template Extraction: MIPs}

The MIP template, $\mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{MIP}}$, is extracted from multiple MIP enhanced regions.
There are three sources of MIPs: electrons, muons, and hadrons.
The MIP $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ shape is extracted individually for each source.
The resulting $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ shapes are consistent across the different background sources.

Electrons, muons, and hadrons exhibit the same $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ shape, indicating that all three background sources are well described by a common MIP $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ template. This can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:MIP_Templates}. The final extracted MIP template is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:MIP_template}.

\subsection{Template Extraction: Fakes}

Fakes do not have a similar $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ shape to other sources of background due to multiple effects. 
The reconstructed path length of fake tracklets is miscalculated because the particles producing the hits do not necessary follow the trajectory of the reconstructed tracklet. 
In addition, fakes can include hits from low-momentum, high- $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ particles that are not typically reconstructed.

These effects result in a unique $\text{d}E/\text{d}x$ shape that must be extracted separately from other background sources. The fake template, $\mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{fake}}$, is extracted from a fake enriched region defined by the selections in Tab.~\ref{tab:fake_enriched_region}. The resulting template is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fake_template}.

\section{Results and Conclusion}


The current projected sensitivities for 1 ns \stau and \chinoonepm benchmark models are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}. Both the ABCD and template-based background estimation methods demonstrate good closure in the validation regions, providing confidence in the robustness of the background prediction. Studies of the correlation between \dedx and \pt show no significant dependence, validating the key assumption underlying the ABCD method and supporting its application in the signal region. 
These studies provide the basis for a search extending sensitivity to charged long-lived particles in the 1 ns lifetime regime.
