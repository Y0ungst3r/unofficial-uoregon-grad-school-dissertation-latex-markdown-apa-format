\chapter{Experimental Upgrades}


The highest peak integrated luminosity of the ATLAS detector to date was $\mathcal{L} = 2.61 \times 10^{34}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, which is beyond its original design specifications from the early 2000s for luminosity of 
$\mathcal{L} = 10^{34}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$.
As the LHC has steadily increased its instantaneous and integrated luminosity, the ATLAS detector has required a series of upgrades to cope with higher data rates, increased detector occupancy, and elevated radiation levels.

Beginning in approximately 2030, the LHC will enter the High-Luminosity phase (HL-LHC). 
During this period, the instantaneous luminosity is expected to increase to approximately
\[
\mathcal{L} = 5 - 7.5 \times 10^{34}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}\mathrm{s}^{-1}.
\]
At this luminosity, an average of approximately 200 proton--proton interactions per bunch crossing is anticipated, compared to the typical 20--50 interactions observed during run 2. 
This dramatic increase in pileup presents significant challenges for event reconstruction, triggering, and data acquisition.

To meet these challenges and continue exploring the particle physics phenomena at the HL-LHC, the ATLAS detector is undergoing extensive upgrades. 
These upgrades are designed to improve radiation tolerance, increase detector granularity, enhance readout bandwidth, and maintain or improve physics performance under extreme operating conditions. 
Major upgrade components include a complete replacement of the ID with a new all-silicon Inner Tracker (ITk), upgrades to the Muon Spectrometer, new front-end and readout electronics for the calorimeters, and substantial improvements to the trigger and data acquisition systems.

The ATLAS upgrade program is divided into two phases. 
Phase 1 upgrades were completed during the Long Shutdown 2 period and commissioned by 2024. 
Phase 2 upgrades are scheduled for completion by 2030, prior to the start of HL-LHC operations.



\section{Phase 1 upgrades}
The Phase 1 upgrade program focused on improving detector performance and trigger capabilities in preparation for run 3 of the LHC. 
Key components of this phase included upgrades to the Muon Spectrometer, the introduction of new trigger electronics, and improvements to detector readout systems. 
These upgrades enabled ATLAS to operate efficiently at higher luminosities and increased event rates during run 3.



\section{Phase 2 upgrades}


The Phase 2 upgrade program is designed to prepare the ATLAS detector for sustained operation during the HL-LHC era and is scheduled for completion in 2030. 
Phase 2 will introduce significant upgrades to the ATLAS trigger and data acquisition system, on/off detector electronics upgrades for LAr Calorimeter, Tile Calorimeters, and the muon detectors. 
Phase 2 will also include a new tracking detector, High Granularity Timing Detector and Muon Chambers.  
The combined effects of integrated luminosity and the planned increase of instantaneous luminosity result in the ID no longer being sufficient for tracking and vertexing in ATLAS beyond run 3. Thus, an integral part of the phase 2 upgrades is the replacement of the ID with ITk. 
% The ID is being replaced with a new all-silicon Inner Tracker (ITk).

The ITk is designed to accommodate the 200 proton-proton collisions per bunch crossing expected at the HL-LHC. 
To meet these demands, the detector features substantially higher granularity, enhanced radiation hardness, and a 1~MHz readout capability.


%It is designed to maintain excellent tracking and vertexing performance in the presence of extreme pileup and radiation levels expected at the HL-LHC. 
The design and performance of the ITk are described in detail in the ATLAS Technical Design Reports~\cite{ATLASITkTDR1,ATLASITkTDR2}, and the ITk forms the primary focus of this chapter.


 
\section{ATLAS ITk}



The primary goal of the ITk upgrade is to maintain or improve charged-particle tracking performance in the high-luminosity environment of the HL-LHC. 
Two major challenges must be addressed to achieve this goal: the substantially increased detector occupancy due to high pileup, and the extreme radiation levels expected over the lifetime of the detector.

The innermost layers of the ITk are expected to experience radiation fluences exceeding
$2 \times 10^{16}~\mathrm{n_{eq}/cm^2}$
and total ionizing doses of up to approximately 1.7 Grad. 
To operate reliably under these conditions, the ITk employs radiation hard silicon sensor technologies, advanced readout electronics, and a detector layout optimized for performance and longevity.
The first layer of the ITk is also designed to be replaceable, allowing for potential replacement during the HL-LHC era. 

ITk is split into two main systems: the Pixel Detector and the Strip Detector.
A brief overview of the Strip detector will be given then the rest of this section will focus on the Pixel Detector as this is the area of ITk that the majority of the work for this dissertation was done. 


\subsection{ITk Strips}
The ITk Strips surrounds the Pixel Detector and provides at least 4 hits for charged particles traversing the detector within a pseudorapidity range of $|\eta| < 2.7$.
ITk Strips will start at about a radius of 400 mm and extend to 1000 mm and have $165m^2$ of silicon micro-strip sensors.
The Strip Detector is made up of overlapping silicon strip sensors ITk Strips is designed to keep occupancy at $\sim1\%$ during the HL-LHC runs.



\subsection{ITk Pixels}


ITk Pixels is made from two systems, the Inner System and the Outer System. 
The outer three layers of the ITk Pixels are called the Outer system. 
The innermost two layers of ITk Pixels are called the Inner System.
The location of specific layer and pixel size for the Inner System can be found in Tab. \ref{tab:barrel_locations} and \ref{tab:ring_locations}.
The basis of the whole ITk pixel detector is the pixel module, which detects the passage of charged particles.
The pixel module is made of a silicon detector bonded directly to the front-end electronics for readout. 
The front-end electronics are designed by the RD53 collaboration, which has designed a front-end for ATLAS and CMS.
The RD53 front-end being used for the Pixel Detectors is called the ITkPixV2.
The pixel modules are built as either quad modules, 4 ITkPixV2 front-ends bump bonded to a single silicon sensor, or as triplets, 3 RD53 front-ends each bonded together to individual silicon sensors, as can be seen in Fig. \ref{fig:itk_modules}. 
The pixel modules will be attached to local supports either rings or staves made of carbon fiber laminates and high thermal-conductive foam core with titanium pipes traversing the center for cooling. 
Cooling for ITk is provided by a  \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) evaporative cooling system that is designed to maintain the temperature of the silicon sensors at -35$^\circ$C to mitigate radiation damage effects and prolong sensor life. 
These rings and staves are mounted into shells that will provide outer structure for ITk as can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:itk_shells}.

The US ATLAS program is responsible for the two innermost layers of Pixel Detector called the Inner System. 
Assembly of the Inner System is being done at SLAC National Laboratory with major contributions from several universities and national labs all over the US. 
To facilitate this assembly a robust and efficient setup for module testing and quality assurance was needed at SLAC. 
Given the nature of ITk, once installed the detector will be inaccessible making it critical to ensure that all systems are fully functional before installation. 
To meet this requirement extensive testing is performed at each stage of the assembly process.

%Components
The construction of the Inner Tracker (ITk) requires many components to be designed, manufactured, and assembled correctly.
The components that make up the Inner System are the mechanical supports, the services, and the modules themselves. 


The mechanical support structure consists of two main elements: local supports and shells. 
Each local support is constructed from a carbon-fiber laminate with a high thermal-conductivity foam core. 
These materials were chosen due to their high strength-to-weight ratio and good thermal conductivity. 
The low weight is important to the mission of ATLAS, as it minimizes the amount of material interactions that high energy particles undergo as they traverse the detector. 
The high thermal conductivity is important as effectively cooling the modules will reduce radiation damage and prolong the effective life of the detector. 

The local supports include staves, which are straight structures used in the barrel region, and rings, which are circular structures used in the end-cap regions. 
Detector modules are directly bonded to the local supports using a thermally compound, SE4445.
Although not actually a glue or adhesive, SE4445 provides the necessary adhesive to keep the modules precisely in place. 
SE445 also provides good thermal conductivity and is sufficiently radiation tolerant to ensure that its properties do not significantly degrade under the high ionizing radiation doses expected at the HL-LHC.

A titanium cooling pipe runs through the center of each support structure, delivering evaporative \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) cooling directly to the detector modules.

The local supports are mounted within larger quarter shell structures that provide mechanical stability and define the overall geometry of the Inner System.
As the name suggests, each quarter shell is one quarter of the cylindrical structure. 
There are three sections that will be made in the Inner System, the inner barrel and two end-caps.
A CAD model of a quarter shell for the barrel and one end-cap can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:itk_shells}. 
The barrel and end-cap structures are not rigidly constrained with respect to one another in the z-direction. 
The complete Inner System is supported such that it remains free to accommodate thermal and mechanical movements along the beam axis.

All electrical power and data lines are called services. 
Type 0 services route the power, DCS, and data via flex cables from the modules to patch panels (PP0).
The PP0s are mounted directly on the quarter shells and have several unique designs depending on their location and on whether they service quad or triplet modules.
Type 1 services run from the PP0s to the edge of the detector with twinaxial cables. 
At the end of type 1 services the data is converted into optical signals, this is done outside the main detector volume due to the lack of radiation hard digital to optical converters. 

Additional services are present further along the service chain, as illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:itk_services}, but these components lie outside the Inner System and are therefore beyond the scope of this section.

%Overview of integration process
The first step of integration is the called ``loading''. 
The process of loading involves bonding the modules directly to the local supports using the thermal compound SE4445.
This process is of the utmost importance as it ensures that the modules are precisely loaded with only 25 microns between modules. 
It is also important to ensure that the modules are able to dissipate heat effectively to the local supports, ensuring reduced radiation damage and longevity of the sensors.

Before loading the modules are extensively tested. 
Testing begins at the production sites where they validate the functionality of the modules.
Once at SLAC the modules are visually inspected and electronically tested to ensure no damage occurred during shipping. 

The local supports likewise are tested before loading. 
Again once shipped to SLAC they are tested visually and then using a CMM they are measured for flatness.


The loaded local supports then will be placed in a test box and cooled to -35$^\circ$C. 
The test boxes were designed and partially constructed as part of the work contributing to this dissertation. Their design was driven by several key requirements: minimizing the internal volume to allow rapid drying of the enclosed air and thereby preventing condensation, providing electrical feedthroughs for power and data connections, accommodating different stave and ring geometries, and incorporating interlock systems to protect both operators and equipment. The final designs are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:boxes}.

The stave test box employs two mirrored PP0 boards that accommodate power and data connections for all stave variants. To achieve this, the staves must be mounted in one of three distinct orientations. The required orientation depends on whether the flex-cable connector is located on the upper or lower side of the flex. This distinction reflects the cable-routing constraints of the detector. The 8 varieties of staves can be seen in Fig. \ref{fig:staves}

The ring test box requires three PP0 boards to accommodate the different types of rings. Due to the different angular locations of the cooling and data lines for different rings, the simplest solution for connecting all required services for testings was to fix all cooling line connections at a single location while allowing the PP0 boards to move. Consequently, the PP0 boards are mounted on a track that permits motion through a 120$^\circ$ arc around the ring, enabling all possible rings to be tested in one box.

To connect the cooling lines to the loaded local supports a connection will have to be made to the delicate titanium cooling pipes. 
This connection was developed at Argonne National Laboratory and is done by using a Yor-Lok like fitting with a special soft ferrule.
Normally with a Yor-Lok fittings the tubing is compressed and deformed to create a tight seal against the ferrule and fittings. 
In this case the ferrule is made of a soft metal and deforms to create a tight seal against the titanium pipe without deforming the titanium pipe. 
This connection allows the local supports to be cooled to the operating temperature of -35$^\circ$C while in the dry box.

While at -35$^\circ$C the local supports will undergo a series of tests to ensure that the modules are fully functional after loading.
This test is imperative as it is the last chance to test the modules before the cooling pipes are welded together and the quarter shells are shipped to CERN.
After testing the local supports are mounted in the shells the titanium cooling pipes are orbital welded together. 
The fully assembled shell will then be shipped to CERN for installation in ATLAS. 







%Delamination testing.
Due to thermal expansion and contraction during detector operation, the risk of mechanical damage to detector modules during thermal cycling was a major concern.

This concern is particularly relevant because the thermal coefficient of expansion (TCE) of carbon fiber can be as low as \(-1.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\), whereas silicon has a TCE of approximately \(16.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\). 
As a result, silicon expands with increasing temperature while carbon fiber may contract, leading to a significant mismatch in thermal expansion behavior.
This mismatch in TCE has the potential to induce mechanical stress at the interface between the detector modules and the local supports, which could lead to delamination during repeated thermal cycling. 
To address this concern, a dedicated thermal cycling test was developed in which a pixel module was bonded to a representative local support structure and subjected to repeated temperature variations.


For this test, a pixel module was glued to a piece of carbon fiber foam made from the same material used in production local supports. 
Stycast epoxy was used as the glue for this study. 
Although SE4445 is used in the final detector assembly, it was not employed for this test because it is initially very soft and becomes stiffer only after significant radiation exposure. 
Stycast epoxy is more rigid than SE4445 that has received the radiation dose expected in the HL-LHC, and therefore provides a conservative, worst-case scenario for evaluating thermal cycling robustness.


Once mounted, the module was thermally cycled between \(-55^\circ\)C and \(60^\circ\)C for a total of 100 cycles. 
Due to the design and stack of the module it expected that any damage induced by thermal cycling would most likely manifest as broken or disconnected bump bonds between the silicon sensor and front ends which we call delamination. 
To assess potential damage, a bump-bond scan was performed following the thermal cycling procedure.
The bump-bond test operates by individually checking each pixel for capacitive coupling to its neighboring pixels. 
Charge is injected into the neighboring pixels surrounding the pixel under test, and the response of the central pixel is then measured. 
If a bump bond is broken, the pixel under test registers no signal. 
If the bump bond is intact, capacitive coupling between neighboring pixels produces a small but measurable charge, indicating a functional connection.

Results from the first 100 thermal cycles can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:delamination_test} and Tab.~\ref{tab:delamination_test}.
After the 100 thermal cycles 380 of the 460,800 total pixels changed to a disconnected status when compared to the baseline scan.
The results show that the number of disconnected bump bonds at the start is roughly the same as the number of disconnected bump bonds after 100 thermal cycles, showing that delamination is not likely to occur during ITk operation in ATLAS.
This test was continued to 1000 thermal cycles and the results were consistent with the 100 cycle test, showing no significant increase in disconnected bump bonds after 1000 thermal cycles.
